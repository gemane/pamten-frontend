Privacy Policy

Last updated: 9 August 2026

Owlgraph maps who owns and controls companies, using public registers. This policy explains what we do with personal data. It has two parts, because two very different groups of people are affected:

  • Part 1 — people who create an Owlgraph account. Data you give us directly (GDPR Art. 13).
  • Part 2 — people who appear in the ownership data. Data about you that we obtained from public registers, not from you (GDPR Art. 14). If you are a company director or a person with significant control, this is the part that concerns you.

What we deliberately do not collect. No tracking pixels, no advertising, no profiling, no third-party scripts of any kind. We do not log your IP address or browser against your account or your sessions. We do not sell or rent data to anyone, and we never will.

Counting, without watching

We do count some things, and we would rather say exactly what than hide behind the word "analytics". We keep running totals: how often a company name has been searched for, how often a search found nothing, how often a feature was used, and how long requests took. That is how we decide which registers to add next, rather than guessing.

What makes it counting rather than watching is that nothing identifies you. There is no user id, no account link, no session id, no stored IP address, no device identifier, no browser string, and no timestamp on any individual event. There is no event log at all — only totals. Nobody, ourselves included, can reconstruct what any one person searched for, because that information is never written down. It is measured in our own database; no analytics company is involved and there is no third-party script in the page.

One thing to be straight about: the counter is keyed on the words searched for, and somebody will type a person's name into a search box. That name is then stored as text, unattached to whoever searched for it. It is readable only by an administrator, and a query nobody has repeated for twelve months is deleted. We would rather tell you that than claim the store is anonymous in every direction.

Cookies

One cookie: an httpOnly session cookie set when you sign in, so you stay signed in without the app having to hold a long-lived credential where JavaScript could reach it. It is strictly necessary to provide the login you asked for, so no consent banner is required and there is nothing to opt out of other than not signing in. It expires after 30 days, and signing out revokes it immediately.

How long we keep it

  • Account data — until you delete your account.
  • Sessions — 30 days maximum, or immediately on sign-out.
  • Sign-in tokens — 15 minutes.
  • Usage totals — deleted once untouched for 12 months.

Deleting your account

Settings → Delete account, or write to us. See the deletion page for the full procedure. Deletion permanently removes your account record together with your password hash, two-factor secret and recovery codes, all your sessions, and your rate-limit counters.

Reports you filed about incorrect company data are anonymised rather than deleted: the report is about a company, not about you, and removing it would silently rewrite the moderation history. Only the link back to you is severed, so the report can no longer be connected to you.

Part 2 — If you appear in the ownership data

Owlgraph publishes a graph of company ownership. Because companies are ultimately owned and controlled by people, that graph names individuals. We did not get this data from you. We took it from public registers that are required by law to publish it. GDPR Art. 14 obliges us to tell you this proactively, which is what this part does.

Where it comes from

SourceWhat we take
UK Companies House — People with Significant Control (PSC) Name, nationality, month and year of birth, country of residence and the companies you control, with the size and nature of that control
US SEC EDGAR Names of officers, directors and insiders disclosed in company filings, and their roles and disclosed holdings
Wikidata Names, and where publicly recorded, dates and places of birth of people notable enough to have an entry

How precise a date of birth is depends on the register. Companies House publishes month and year only, and that is all we hold for a UK PSC. Wikidata records a full date for people who have an entry there, and we do hold that — because the exact date is what stops two people who share a name being treated as one. Without it, a company you have nothing to do with could be shown as yours.

We do not display the full date. For a living person the panel shows an age, and their career timeline is marked with the year of birth. The day and month — the part that identifies someone, or answers their bank's security question — stay unpublished. The year is there because it is what makes a career legible: a first directorship at 24 means something different from one at 54, and a timeline without it is a list of dates against no one. For someone who has died we show the years of birth and death, because those show the period in which that person could have held control — which is what an ownership record is read for.

We do not hold your home address, your national identifiers, or any financial account details.

Why we do it, and on what basis

The legal basis is Art. 6(1)(f) — legitimate interests. The interest is transparency of corporate ownership: making it possible to see who ultimately controls a company, which is the express purpose for which these registers were made public in the first place, and which supports journalism, research, due diligence and anti-money-laundering work.

We have weighed that interest against your rights, and the balance rests on these points: the data is already published by law and expected to be public; we take the minimum the registers disclose about individuals; we do not enrich it with anything private; we do not profile, score, advertise to or make automated decisions about anyone; and control of a company is information about a public role, not about private life.

You can ask us for the reasoning behind this assessment, and we will provide it.

Who else sees it

The ownership graph is publicly accessible — that is the point of the service, and you should assume anything in it can be read by anyone. Beyond publication on this site, we do not share person data with anyone. We do not sell it, and we do not send it to advertisers or data brokers.

How long we keep it

For as long as the source register publishes it and the record remains relevant to understanding who controls a company. Where a register marks a person's control as ended, we keep the historical record with its end date, because a change of ownership is itself the information people come here for. If you object successfully (below), we remove or suppress the record regardless.

Your rights, and how to use them

You have the right to access the data we hold about you, to have inaccurate data corrected, to request erasure, to restrict processing, to data portability, and — because we rely on legitimate interests — to object at any time on grounds relating to your particular situation. If you object, we must stop unless we can show compelling legitimate grounds that override your rights.

Two routes:

  • Report an incorrect record directly. Every company and person in the app has a report button. It works without an account, and reports go to a moderation queue. Best for "this is wrong" or "this is not me".
  • Write to {{OWNER_EMAIL}} for access, erasure, objection, or anything needing a considered answer. We reply within one month, as the GDPR requires. Please describe which record you mean; we may need to ask a question to be sure we have the right person, which is itself a safeguard against someone else editing your record.

One honest limitation. If we remove a record that the source register still publishes, our next import can bring it back. When we act on an objection we suppress the record so re-import cannot resurrect it. If the underlying entry is itself wrong, it is worth correcting it at the register too — otherwise every other user of that register keeps the error.

Security

Everything travels over HTTPS. Passwords are stored as bcrypt hashes and session tokens only as SHA-256 hashes, so neither can be read back out of the database. Sessions rotate on use, and a replayed session token invalidates the whole family of tokens it belongs to. Two-factor authentication is available and we recommend it.
